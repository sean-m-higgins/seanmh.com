b-card QR Kit

Four scannable codes for the Card version of the site — one plain, three styled to the site's indigo. Sized for print, and read back by two independent decoders at every size from 110 to 400 pixels before any of them made this page.

What they encode

url https://seanmh.com/card

/card is a short path the Worker owns: it redirects to ?v=b-card, so a scan lands on the Card version specifically rather than whatever a returning visitor's cookie last selected. It is short enough to say out loud, and it encodes into a 29×29 symbol instead of the 33×33 the query string needed — a 12% larger module at any given sticker size, which is exactly the margin that matters at one inch.

The redirect is a 302, not a 301. These get printed on objects that outlive routing decisions, and a permanent redirect would sit in the cache of every browser that ever scanned an old sticker.

How they were checked

Two independent decoders read every file: a ZXing-derived one, whose lineage most phone scanners share, and a strict geometric one. Both were run at every size from 110 to 400 pixels in 5-pixel steps — 59 sizes each — plus blur, dim, skew and print-noise passes. The SVGs were then re-rendered through a real browser and swept again, to confirm the vector files carry identical geometry to the PNGs.

Variant ZXing-derived reader Strict geometric reader Verdict
qr-mono 59 / 59 59 / 59 clean sweep
qr-indigo 59 / 59 55 / 59 clean on both in print sizes
qr-indigo-glyph 59 / 59 55 / 59 clean on both in print sizes
qr-dark 59 / 59 0 / 59 inversion only

About those three misses. The strict reader drops the styled codes at 150, 160 and 170 pixels, plus an isolated 235, and reads them at every one of the other 55 sizes. The same reader has its own scattered dead sizes on the previous, larger version of these codes too — it is a quirk of how that library samples certain scale ratios, not something about the artwork, and it does not correspond to anything a camera does. A phone sees continuous optics, not a bitmap resampled to an unlucky integer. The ZXing-derived reader has no dead sizes at all.

Worth stating plainly: an earlier version of this page claimed a clean sweep from a six-size sample. A denser sweep found the band. The codes did not change; the measurement got better.

The one asterisk. The strict reader scores zero on qr-dark — but it scores zero on a plain, perfectly ordinary black-and-white code with the colors flipped too. That reader simply cannot invert; it is a limitation of the library, not a defect in the design. The ZXing-derived reader, whose lineage most phone scanners share, reads the dark code under every condition. iOS Camera and Android both handle inverted codes; a few older third-party scanner apps do not.

If a code has to work on an unknown scanner with no chance to test, use qr-mono.

Where the styling budget went

Two findings from sweeping the design parameters against both readers, worth keeping if these ever get restyled:

  • Rounding the data modules is almost free. Even fully circular dots decoded everywhere. The shape of the small squares carries far less weight than it looks like it should.
  • Rounding the three corner eyes is expensive. As the corner radius grows, the strict reader stops recognising the 1 : 1 : 3 : 1 : 1 finder ratio, and it falls off a cliff.

The generator settles at mod_round = 0.35, eye_round = 0.5 — the roundest combination that still holds up on both readers. The first styled attempt used 1.75 and did not scan at all.

About those Apple codes

The round, three-lobed Apple codes are App Clip Codes, and they are not a general-purpose QR alternative. They are generated only by Apple's own App Clip Code Generator, they must be tied to an App Clip registered in App Store Connect, and they point at an app experience rather than a plain web page. There is no way to make one for a personal site, and non-Apple devices cannot read the distinctive part of them at all.

What you can borrow is the idea: a QR code with real color and a deliberate shape is still a completely standard QR code. That is what these are.

Sizing for print

The styled codes are 37 modules wide including their quiet zone. Scan distance is the usual rule of thumb — roughly ten times the code's width — not something measured here.

Assumes the code occupies about 85% of the piece. The hard floor is roughly 18 mm square at a 0.5 mm module; a 1-inch sticker sits comfortably above it.

Handing this to a printer

  • Ask for matte, never gloss or UV. Specular glare off a glossy laminate under direct light is one of the most common ways a perfectly good printed code fails to scan. It costs the same.
  • Specify 100% K for qr-mono, not rich black. Converted naively it becomes a 294%-ink four-plate build; on modules near 1 mm, plate registration error fuzzes every edge. One plate cannot misregister.
  • Expect the indigo to shift. Through a generic CMYK profile #4338ca becomes 87/71/0/0 and prints back around #2f5293 — duller, bluer, most of the violet gone. Contrast against white only moves 7.90 to 7.64, so scanning is unaffected; it is purely cosmetic. Ask for a Pantone if screen-to-print match matters.
  • Keep the white space around the edge. The four-module quiet zone is drawn into every file. Cropping to the code itself is the most common way a working QR stops working.
  • Send the SVG, not the PNG — most shops want vector, and the PNGs top out near 4.4 in at 300 dpi.
  • Do not recolor them by hand. The indigo was picked to hold contrast against white. Lighter accents look fine on screen and fail on a phone in a dim room — rerun the generator instead.
  • For qr-dark on dark stock, the shop needs a white underbase to lay light ink down. Not every sticker vendor does it; ask before ordering.

The quiet zone is the safe area. Rather than inset the code inside a separate 3 mm margin, the symbol is sized so its own four-module quiet zone runs out to the trim line. That buys the largest possible module at every finished size, and a die-cut that drifts inward eats quiet zone instead of data — which is what a quiet zone is for. The background then bleeds 3 mm past the trim, so a drift outward never exposes bare paper. On the 2-inch sticker the data sits 5.5 mm inside the cut line, comfortably past the usual 3 mm requirement.

No crop marks, on purpose. The production files set MediaBox, TrimBox and BleedBox properly, which is how a shop's software finds the cut — machine-readable, and it cannot be misread the way a drawn mark can. Most online printers want exactly this. If yours insists on visible marks, say so and I will add a slug area.

They are RGB. Sticker Mule, Moo and similar accept RGB and convert. If your shop wants CMYK supplied, hand them the numbers from the section above rather than letting a default profile decide.
