## The feeling

Summer heat in an open garage. Sawdust in the light. Raw hands, long days that
run into nights, the quiet therapy of sanding. The pride of a thing that is
square, level and yours, and costs only its materials. The film should feel
patient and warm, then land on the deep glow of a finished, stained table with
light passing through its glass.

## Facts (from Sean)

- Every material was bought at Home Depot or Lowe's. (Draw generic store
  aisles and lumber racks, never a store's branding.)
- Sean already had power tools in the garage: power saws, power sanders,
  clamps, a drill, etc.
- It is hard work. Hands are often raw after a session and sore later.
- About **six solid days of work, spread over a few weeks** one summer,
  including long days and some nights.
- Built for himself, so the cost was only the materials: **under $200**.
- **Wood:** mostly **oak**.
- **Size:** about **1 ft tall × 3 ft long × 2 ft wide** (12 × 36 × 24 in).
- **Glass:** about **30 × 14 in**, ordered from a local glass shop, cut to
  the design, and set into a routed rabbet so it sits flush with the wood.
- **Joinery:** wooden dowels and glue; small screws at the corner pieces of
  the lower level, connecting and securing the four legs.
- **Order of finishing:** the table was dry-fit raw, taken apart, **stained in
  pieces**, then assembled and glued for good.
- **Finish:** dark brown stain (the exact colour isn't remembered; sample it
  from IMG_0978 and IMG_1038), then **two coats of polyurethane**.
- No Sunny in this film. ("feat. Sunny" belongs to the Scroll version only;
  he wasn't born when the table was built, though he uses it now.)

## The story (Sean's words, lightly edited; keep its substance)

It all starts with an idea and a design. You have to come up with something
that fits your needs, your requirements, and your skill level. Furniture has to
be designed precisely: every piece of the table is measured out in advance,
and the best way to avoid going back to the store again and again is to put
every measurement into the design and bring it with you.

Once the idea is thought through and the design is finished, with drawings and
a list of what to buy, it's off to a store like Home Depot or Lowe's, which
carries tools, hardware, and the wood itself. At the store you decide which kind
of wood to use and pick out the best-looking boards, because most of them
aren't great. Then follow the list for everything else: screws, nails, glue,
stain, hinges. Forget something? You can always go back. Check out and head
home. Some stores will cut the wood for you, which helps when you aren't
driving a truck.

Back home in the garage, unpack and start on the remaining cuts, measuring
everything at least twice. What comes next depends on what you're building.
Take the coffee table. Its top holds a large pane of glass that no ordinary
store sells, so I found a local glass shop online, called them, and ordered a
pane cut to the exact dimensions of my design.

Then I finished the rest of the cuts and sanded every piece, ready for
assembly. Sanding is painstaking, but it's also therapeutic, and it takes up a
long stretch of the build. The table has two levels: the top, with the glass in
the middle, and a lower shelf for coffee-table books, a video game console and
the like. I started measuring and screwing pieces together, a few and then more
and more. Once the four legs were ready, it was time for the top. Its wood had
to be cut and routed so the glass sits in it, flush with the wood, making the
top one flat surface: glass in the middle, a wood border all the way around.

With that done, the whole table could be dry-fit, but there was still raw
sanded wood, small gaps, and every joint still to fix for good. So I took it
apart and stained each piece a deep dark brown. Then I assembled it one last
time: dowels and glue, small screws to lock the legs to the lower level, and
anything heavy I had as a clamp. Finishing touches smoothed everything out and
made sure it was sturdy, tight and level. Two coats of polyurethane went on to
protect it for the long run. Once it dried, the table was done and could come
indoors.

## The look (choose at the style-frames gate)

Render one style frame of each and let Sean pick:

- **A. Etched coloured pencil** (films-plan's choice; Kengo Works `dew`).
  Hatched graphite and coloured pencil on cream; wood grain drawn line by
  line; colour withheld until the stain beat.
- **B. Warm pastel** (`chalk1-3`). Directional chalk strokes, golden garage
  light, sawdust as pastel dust. More painterly and emotional.
- **C. Etched with one glass moment.** A, but the glass beat switches to a
  refractive reeded-glass rendering (`video-realistic.mov`) for a few seconds
  of near-photographic magic.

**Recommendation:** C. The hand-drawn restraint makes the glass reveal and the
first colour land harder.

**Palette seed:** cream paper `#f3ead8`, graphite with a blue in it `#2b2a4c`,
raw oak `#e2c79a`, oak grain `#c49a64`, stain `#4a2a1c` (refine it from
IMG_0978 / IMG_1038), sawdust `#f0dcae`, glass `#a9c9c4`; accent: sunlight
`#f2b544`.
