## The feeling

A teenager takes on something bigger than he is, with a deadline he can't move
(his 18th birthday). Doubt at the drawing board, then a breakthrough. A
neighbour's welding sparks at night, a family's hands, a driveway turned into a
shop for months. Weight and steel and matte black paint. Then lightness: a
wheelchair rolling up something you built, and a ramp leaving on a trailer to
serve people you will never meet. The film should feel like a coming-of-age
story told through one object. It is the most emotional film of the three.

## The story (Sean's words, lightly edited; keep its substance)

This story spans more than a year, from when I was 16 until I was nearly 18,
the last year a boy can be a Boy Scout and the true deadline for the project.
The ultimate goal in Scouting is to become an Eagle Scout, and one major
requirement is an Eagle Scout project. They come in every shape and size, from
building benches to painting to hosting a blood drive, but they all have to
help others in need. It's often easiest to find an organisation or non-profit
you have a connection to, or a passion for, and ask them directly what would
help their clients most.

I chose a therapeutic equestrian non-profit, where riding horses helps
children and adults, many of them disabled. When I first visited and said I
wanted to do my Eagle Scout project for them, they suggested building shelving
for extra storage in one of the stables. I was looking for something bigger,
so they took some time to think. Then they came back with a challenge, and I
took it: **build a portable wheelchair ramp, so riders could mount the horses
indoors in bad weather.** All their existing ramps were permanent structures,
outdoors in the open air.

I took that challenge home and started designing. I began with the
requirements. The organisation offered some old, unused trailers that could be
pulled around the grounds by a riding mower or ATV. I tried to make a design
work with them, but every version had glaring problems that I felt would make
it fail. So after several attempts I decided to build it from scratch, buying
all the materials as part of the project. Back at the drawing board I sketched
more designs, centred on a few needs: portable, an ADA-accessible ramp,
movable by an ATV hitch, and able to be set up by one woman alone if need be.
Beyond that, the design was mine.

The final design was three separate pieces, each on its own set of wheels,
that connect into one long but safe wheelchair ramp, each piece movable by a
single person with an ATV. I used the math I'd learned in high school,
geometry and trigonometry, to draw out the angles and measurements of the ramp
and to build a list of every material I needed. The design first called for
heavy-duty screws and bolts, but thankfully my neighbour was a welder and
offered his time to help weld the steel structure, which made the design
orders of magnitude better. I took advice from my mother, a civil engineer,
and my uncle, a structural engineer, to make sure it was good and strong.

With the design complete, I moved on to materials. Again, this was months of
work on nights and weekends, after school and sports. I bought from Home
Depot and Harbor Freight, but also from specialty and industrial shops: the
steel framing, angle bent lengthwise into an L (2″×2″×10′, or 4″×10′ flat),
and railing from a fencing company. Another uncle donated sheet metal from his
workplace, and organisations connected to my school and troop donated money.

Then the build began. The first huge job was cutting the ten-foot steel into
the individual pieces that would become the ramp's frame. My dad and I used a
Sawzall with special blades, and we went through many blades, to cut the
metal. After weeks, every cut was done. Next we laid out and measured every
piece so it was ready for the welder. Day after day, section after section,
the pieces were welded together and the frame took shape. He even showed me
how to weld. After that we attached the axles for the wheels and the hitch
connections, the last welds.

The project was really coming together, but there was a lot more to do. We
cleaned the frame with wire brushes and painted it all matte black. Then came
the floors, plywood and then sheet metal over it. Then the railings, and the
finishing touches like traction strips on the ramp. And that was it: a fully
formed, portable wheelchair ramp! I tested it with a wheelchair in my
driveway, then delivered it in pieces on two trailers to the organisation and
left it with them to help their future clients. The project was a massive
success. I learned so much, and I gave something of real value to an
organisation in need.

## The look (choose at the style-frames gate)

Render one style frame of each and let Sean pick:

- **A. Cut paper and relief** (`layered1-4`, recommended). Charcoal and cream
  paper layers with real cast shadows; matte-black steel as the darkest
  layer; the molten-orange accent reserved for **welding sparks**. Layers give
  the weight of steel and the depth of a long build.
- **B. Field notebook** (films-plan's choice). Pencil, watercolour wash,
  `tornPath` paper scraps and tape: a teenager's project binder, memory-like.
- **C. Notebook into relief.** B for acts I–II (the planning), switching to A
  once the steel arrives (acts III–V): from idea on paper to a real thing.

**Palette seed:** cream `#efe7d6`, charcoal `#2a2b31`, steel grey `#8d9096`,
galvanised sheet `#c9cdd2`, cedar post `#b98a5a`, grass `#6f8f4e`, blueprint
navy `#1f2f52`; accent: weld orange `#ff7a1a`.
